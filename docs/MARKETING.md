# Hot Boy & Cool Girl: How to Sell the Game

*Started 2026-10-24. Nothing here is decided yet (💬). The plan is to finish the game first, but to start sharing it early.*

## 1. How we make money 💬
**No ads.** The owner hates them, and they break P5 (friends playing in 30 seconds).

**Recommended model: free to try, pay once, and only one friend pays.**
- **Free:** Chapter 1 (or the first 1–2 levels) for everyone.
- **One purchase (about $3–5)** unlocks all chapters, modes and the level designer, forever.
- **Friend's pass:** if the owner creates the room, the friend plays the paid parts for free. Only one of the two has to buy. This is how "It Takes Two" sells, and every buyer brings a new player.
- **Where to ask for money:** right after the most exciting moment, when players want more. The end of Chapter 1 is a good spot.
- **Payment:** through Google Play / App Store in the Capacitor app. No server needed, so the $0 running cost limit (L2) holds.
- **The website** stays as the free demo that leads to the app.
- **Later:** cosmetics (hats, trail colors) that never change gameplay.

## 2. Where we sell
1. **Mobile first** (Android, then iPhone): the game is built for phones.
   - Google Play costs $25 once and needs 12 testers for 14 days.
   - The App Store costs $99 a year and uses TestFlight for testing.
2. **Steam later:** co-op puzzle games sell well there.
   - Needs keyboard or controller support and a $100 fee.
   - A friend can join through Steam Remote Play Together without owning the game.

## 3. When to start selling
Not when "everything" is built. Start selling when **a stranger who plays the free chapter wants to pay for the next one.** That is something we measure, not guess.

**Milestone before selling:**
- A polished free Chapter 1 that strangers love
- About 4–6 paid chapters
- The "different eyes" and password-gate mechanics built in
- A simple story
- Two-phone online play that works smoothly over the internet (not only home Wi-Fi)

## 4. What a marketing expert checks (in order)
1. **The hook in one sentence.** For example: "Two friends, two phones, one temple: fire and water must save each other." If it can't be said in one line, it can't be advertised.
2. **The 5-second clip.** Short gameplay videos (TikTok, Reels, YouTube Shorts) sell games like this. A funny co-op death or a clever "aha" moment works better than a polished trailer.
3. **Test with strangers before building more.** About 20–50 people who don't know us play the free chapter. Watch three numbers:
   - **How many finish Chapter 1.**
   - **How many come back another day.**
   - **How many invite a friend.**

   If these are low, more levels won't help; fix the fun first.
4. **Wishlists or pre-registration.** These show whether people want the game.
   - On Steam, about 7,000+ wishlists before launch is a good sign.
   - On mobile, use Google Play pre-registration.
5. **Build an audience months before launch.**
   - Post development clips and make a Discord.
   - Share early versions.
   - Most successful indie games start this about a year before release.
6. **The store page.** The icon, the first 3 screenshots and a short video decide whether someone installs.
7. **Price and the pay-wall moment** (see section 1).
8. **Launch and press.** Send the game to YouTubers and streamers who play co-op games; co-op is very streamable.

## 5. What makes the ads work (the owner's ideas)
- **Tell a story:** a simple story and lore sell better than mechanics alone.
- **Show the graphics:** each chapter gets its own background with the layered depth look (doc 9.1).
- **Show the mechanics:** short clips of the best co-op moments.
- **Say it's free to try:** the first levels or chapter are free; pay for the next chapters, modes or the level designer.

## 6. Honest note
Most indie games earn little. What makes a co-op game spread is **people inviting their friends**, so the free first chapter, the friend's pass and the instant room link matter more than the price.
